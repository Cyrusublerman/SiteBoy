The ambiguity and detached nature of written text has become a burden of
the modern era. Constant conflict is created both directly and indirectly by a
lack of many human qualities in text. The inability to assess the non-verbal
qualities of the communication leaves the reader without the true sense of the
emotional, human qualities of the author (James 2017). This in turn can lead the
reader to respond and act in a manner which may contradict the real-world, face-
to-face reaction they would have if witnessing the author speak this text.
Through computationally processing the spoken word, the CHAT team aims
to provide insight into potential ways written text can evolve to become a truer
representation of spoken language.
How does the medium in which the text is viewed affect the reader's emotional
connection to the speech? If the text is formed into a physical artifact will the
reader attribute different emotional qualities to the text? How can the typography
be altered to represent the nonverbal aspects of human speech?
These questions and more were explored through research and experimentations
over the course of the semester, resulting in the development of CHAT - a
device which computationally humanises typography through audio to text
translation. Using technologies such as speech to text, electromagnetic field
recording and audio analysis, the team mapped non-verbal language to the textual
representation of the verbal. This was then built into a zoomorphically designed
object that prints these messages (along with an image of the sender) on thermal
paper to create a timeline of conversation that now occupies the physical world as
a receipt of the interaction.
We hope that this device illustrates the potential for further development in the
text-based world, especially given the move to a greater online presence.
