The environments which facilitate optimum wellbeing and mental ability seem to be those which reproduce and present the mathematical and structural properties and relations of those which encompassed ancestral humanity. As such, the core aspects of the environments which facilitated the development of homo sapiens may still be needed for ideal cognitive and emotional function, even in today's hyper-synthetic world (Kellert, 2008).

Furthermore, analysis of the mathematical composition of nature has shown insight into what forms an ideal environment. Mimicking the key components and relations of natural forms can bring the natural elements into otherwise purely synthetic environments (Salingaros, 2012). These mathematical compositions and patterns have a direct influence on the neurology of the experiencer, potentially through the modulation of pleasure-regulating opioid receptors (Biederman & Vessel, 2006).

Biophilic design, especially within the architectural space, seeks to reconnect modern society to the natural world. This goal, however, has been hindered by numerous challenges both internal and external to the design community (Kellert, 2008). The first and potential core issue appears to be a lack of clarity of the specific aim of biophilic design (Zhong, Schröder & Bekkering, 2022). What does it mean to reconnect modern society to the natural world? To answer this, firstly there needs to be a deep understanding of how humans evolved to connect with nature.

It is only then that adjustments can be made based on the highlighted differences between then and now. Once variances have been identified, precise mechanisms must be considered to ensure the responses are not misguided or vague. For example, to identify the mechanisms by which humans interact with the natural and synthetic worlds, you need to understand the mechanisms of human physiology and psychology so that any manipulation of the human-environment paradigm will not be detrimental. This, though becoming increasingly complex already, is only the beginning, as it is also important to consider how modern society is formed, how people currently live and what the current state of design is.

This places designers and scientists in a position in which they must adopt what Richard P. Feynman referred to as a *"satisfactory philosophy of ignorance."* A realisation that, whilst humbling, has facilitated enormous progress. Like many other complex areas, the answer probably does not lie in a direct and linear interrogation of the problems but instead in a multifaceted and multidirectional inquisition into the intricacies of what biophilic design could be and how it may function.

Neuroscience research has shown a direct connection between the shapes and forms of a biophilic or natural space and the visual processing areas of the human brain (Bratman, Hamilton & Daily, 2012). The specific curvilinear patterns of the stimuli appear to activate specialised neuronal systems that may be present to identify resources and environments that facilitate survival and reproduction. This ingrained value of aesthetics has been noted in several studies, whereby respondents report a sense of order and feelings of visual ease after viewing (Sternberg & Wilson, 2006).

Another observed entangling of visual stimuli and cognitive behaviour has been the direct but abstract eliciting of visual memory. The organised complexity of natural patterns appears to provoke the viewer to search through their memory in hopes of identifying novel stimuli. This searching is the foundation of intrigue and provokes positive feedback and feelings of beauty and joy.

Together, these connections between natural stimuli and cognitive response illustrate potential mechanisms for biophilic design's ability to increase wellbeing and mental wellness.

## Christopher Alexander: 15 Fundamental Properties of Living Structure

*(from Christopher Alexander, [*The Nature of Order, Book 1: The Phenomenon of Life*](https://www.natureoforder.com/no1/phenomenon.htm))*

| Property | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| Levels of Scale | Coherent hierarchy of sizes, from small to large. |
| Strong Centers | Clear focal points that strengthen the whole. |
| Boundaries | Defined edges that both separate and connect spaces. |
| Alternating Repetition | Rhythmic repetition with variation. |
| Positive Space | Every part of space is shaped and defined, not leftover. |
| Good Shape | Geometric clarity and harmony across parts. |
| Local Symmetries | Symmetry in smaller regions, not just globally. |
| Deep Interlock & Ambiguity | Overlapping and interwoven elements that reinforce each other. |
| Contrast | Differences that strengthen the perception of order. |
| Gradients | Smooth, directional transitions (size, density, intensity). |
| Roughness | Imperfection and variation that feel natural and alive. |
| Echoes | Similarities of form and proportion repeated across scales. |
| The Void | A still centre or empty core that balances complexity. |
| Simplicity & Inner Calm | Clarity, minimalism, absence of clutter. |
| Not-Separateness | Wholeness; parts feel connected to their surroundings. |

### Notes for Design Justification

- The **Terrapin patterns** provide practical, design-oriented heuristics that can be embedded into architectural and product design.
- The **Alexander properties** offer a deeper, structural logic for why things feel natural and alive.
- Together they establish the theoretical framework underpinning this project: bridging environmental neuroscience, mathematical structure, and biophilic design into tangible, generative form.
