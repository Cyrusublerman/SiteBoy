Built geometry influences cognition and affect. Evidence from environmental neuroscience indicates that environments expressing natural order can support restoration and well-being. Synthetic Biophilia develops a reproducible method for embedding that order into design by translating natural growth rules into spatial and structural logic.

Using phyllotaxis as the generative model, the approach isolates the governing relations of plant formation and maps them onto a domed field of points. From this field it derives coherent lattices, joints and surface attachments that preserve the proportional rhythms and perceived vitality characteristic of living systems—without resorting to figurative mimicry. The result is a dome that behaves like nature in its organisation, rather than merely resembling it.

More broadly, the project proposes a methodology for applying natural geometries to architecture: extract the mathematics of formation, parameterise it for design control, and enforce constraints that keep the resulting assemblies legible, fabricable and hollow where required. The outcome is not a toolchain but a transferable way of working that connects biophilic theory, environmental neuroscience, and parametric geometry to produce environments tuned to human well-being.
